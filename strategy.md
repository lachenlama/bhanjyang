# Bhanjyang — what this could become

Written August 2026, against the live site at bhanjyang.vercel.app.

This is a strategy document, not a pitch deck. Where I think an idea is wrong I say
so, and where numbers are guesses I label them as guesses.

---

## 1. What you actually have

Be precise about this, because it determines everything else.

You do **not** have a music product. You have no rights, no catalog, no licences,
and no ability to stop YouTube from breaking your player tomorrow. Spotify would
not buy this.

What you have is rarer and more useful:

1. **A distribution asset aimed at a community that is badly served online.** The
   Gorkha/Nepali hills diaspora has money, homesickness, and almost nothing made
   for them that isn't a Facebook group or a WhatsApp forward.
2. **An emotional trigger with a share mechanic already built.** The dedication
   link is a WhatsApp-native object. This audience runs on WhatsApp.
3. **Taste.** The site looks like it was made by someone from there, for people
   from there. That is the whole moat. It cannot be cloned by someone who has
   never been cold in Darjeeling in July.

So: **the music is the door, not the room.** Every plan below is about what
happens after somebody walks through it.

## 2. The fork in the road

You described three different companies in one paragraph. They need different
teams, different money, and different lives. Pick one as the trunk.

| | **A. The archive** | **B. The shop** | **C. The marketplace** |
|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | The definitive cultural home of hill Nepali music & memory | A curated store selling ~20–40 things from the hills | A platform where any local maker lists and sells |
| Revenue | Grants, patronage, sponsorship | Product margin (40–60%) | Commission (8–15%) |
| Hard part | Staying interesting | Logistics, taste | Cold start, both sides |
| Realistic year-1 | ₹2–8L | ₹6–25L | ~₹0 |
| Fails when | You get bored | You buy the wrong stock | Nobody lists, or nobody buys |
| My read | Foundation for everything | **Start here** | Not yet, maybe never |

### Why not the marketplace

This is where I disagree with you, and it's the most important thing in this doc.

A marketplace is a *platform* business. Platforms need liquidity: enough sellers
that buyers find what they want, enough buyers that sellers bother listing. Below
that threshold a marketplace is worse than useless — it's an empty shop with the
lights on, and it burns your credibility with the makers you recruited.

You'd also be signing up for: onboarding sellers who've never sold online,
payment splits, GST on marketplace transactions, shipping from hill towns with
patchy courier coverage, returns, disputes, seller fraud, and customer service in
three languages. That is a full-time operations company. It is not a thing you
add to a music site.

**Build a shop instead.** A shop is retail economics: buy or consign 20 good
things, photograph them beautifully, sell them at a real margin. It works at tiny
scale. It needs exactly the thing you already have — taste. And every supplier
relationship you build for the shop is a seller you'd have needed for a
marketplace anyway. If the shop works and you genuinely can't keep up with
demand, *that* is when a marketplace earns its existence.

Marketplaces are what shops become. They are not what shops start as.

## 3. The problem you have to fix before selling anything

**The illustrations are AI-generated, and the plan is to sell local art.**

That contradiction will be noticed, and it should be. A site that positions
itself as the home of hill culture, whose own imagery was generated rather than
painted by anyone from the hills, selling art by people from the hills — a
journalist or an artist will put that in a post and they will be right to.

There are also practical problems. In the US, purely AI-generated images get no
copyright protection, so you cannot stop anyone from reselling your prints.
India's position is unsettled. You own the output under OpenAI's terms and can
use it commercially, but "you may use it" is not "you can defend it."

**The fix is also the best business move available to you.** Commission an
illustrator from Darjeeling or Kalimpong to repaint the backgrounds. Pay properly
— ₹15,000–40,000 for a set is realistic and cheap for what it buys you:

- Art you actually own and can sell prints of
- Your first artist relationship and your first shop supplier
- The founding story: *we paid a local artist before we sold anything*
- The moral standing to ask other makers to trust you

Use the AI images as mood boards for the brief. That's what they're good for.
Do this before any commerce goes live, not after.

## 4. The new music section — do it, and understand why it's big

Your instinct is right, but the reason it's right isn't the one you gave.

Gauley Bhai, Bipul Chettri, Albatross, Mantra, Night, Kutumba, Neetesh Jung
Kunwar — these artists are **alive, working, and reachable.** Aruna Lama is not.
That changes everything:

- They **want** promotion. A beautiful site featuring them is free marketing.
- They have official channels, so the licensing posture is clean.
- They have merch, tours, and audiences. Merch is inventory you don't finance.
- They are the fastest introduction to every other maker in the hills.

**The living artists are a business development channel that happens to also be
content.** That is the highest-leverage thing in this entire document.

One structural note. Bipul Chettri and Gauley Bhai are *hills* artists —
Kalimpong, Darjeeling, that diaspora. Albatross, Mantra, Night, Nepathya are
*Kathmandu*. Those are different scenes with different politics, and this site's
identity is hills-first. Don't flatten them into "new Nepali music." Suggested
sections:

- **पहाड** — the hills' own: Bipul Chettri, Gauley Bhai, Kalimpong/Darjeeling acts
- **काठमाडौँ** — the Kathmandu scene
- **पुरानो** — what you have now

You said you want a different illustration style for the newer crowd. Yes — but
different *room*, same *house*. Same type system, same brass, same mist. Change
the scene and the light: night, wet streets, a gig, neon over Darjeeling Motor
Stand. If it looks like a different website, you've split your brand in half.

## 5. Revenue, ranked by return per unit of pain

**Tier 1 — do these first**

1. **Prints of the backgrounds.** Print-on-demand (Printful, or an Indian
   equivalent for domestic shipping). Zero inventory, ships worldwide, diaspora
   wall-art demand is real and underserved. Requires §3 to be fixed first.
   *Effort: low. Ceiling: ₹5–15L/yr.*
2. **Patronage.** A quiet "support this" with UPI and an international option.
   Nostalgia is the highest-converting emotion there is, and this costs you one
   afternoon. *Effort: trivial. Ceiling: ₹1–4L/yr. Never gate the music behind it.*
3. **Grants.** Genuinely free money you are currently ignoring. Cultural
   preservation and archive work is fundable: India Foundation for the Arts,
   state cultural boards in Sikkim and West Bengal, British Council, Goethe,
   diaspora foundations. An oral-history archive of elders talking about these
   songs is exactly what these funds exist for.
   *Effort: medium, slow. Ceiling: ₹5–20L, non-dilutive.*

**Tier 2 — once you have traffic**

4. **The shop.** 20–40 curated things: single-estate tea, Dhaka textiles, bamboo,
   thangka-adjacent work, prints, band merch. Start on consignment so you finance
   nothing. *Effort: high. Ceiling: this is the actual business.*
5. **Artist merch as a diaspora storefront.** The bands can't easily ship to
   Sydney or Doha. You can. Revenue share. *Effort: medium.*

**Tier 3 — needs real numbers first**

6. **Sponsorship.** The natural buyers are unusually well-matched: remittance
   companies (Wise, Remitly, Western Union — their entire pitch is "missing
   home"), Darjeeling tea estates, hill tourism boards, Nepali airlines. Real
   budgets, but nobody talks to you below ~50k monthly visits.

**Do not do these**

- **Charging for dedications.** It's the growth engine. Taxing it kills it.
- **Display ads.** Google AdSense would destroy in one afternoon everything the
  design does for you. The audience is small; the atmosphere is the product.
- **Subscriptions for music you don't own.** Legally reckless and morally thin.

## 6. What to build next, concretely

**Now (weeks)**
- Commission the local illustrator (§3). Everything else waits on this.
- New music sections with the hills/Kathmandu split.
- Nepali UI toggle. Currently the interface is bilingual by decoration; make it
  real. Meaningful for the older audience the archive is *about*.
- Song request form live (already wired, needs the URL).
- `localStorage` for scene choice.

**Next (1–2 months)**
- **Song stories, from the community.** This was in the original plan and it is
  still the single most defensible feature: three lines of real memory per song,
  credited to the person who sent it. Nobody can copy that. It also converts
  listeners into contributors, which is how a site becomes a place.
- **Artist pages and interviews.** Editorial is how you earn the right to sell
  someone's work later. Do the journalism before the commerce.
- **Gig calendar** for Darjeeling, Kalimpong, Gangtok. Costs almost nothing,
  makes you useful weekly rather than once, and puts you in every promoter's
  contacts.

**Then (3–6 months)**
- Prints.
- The shop, consignment-only, 20 items maximum.
- Analytics you can defend to a sponsor (Plausible or similar — privacy-first,
  consistent with "no tracking, no accounts").

**Deliberately not yet**
- Accounts and logins. Your best feature works because it needs neither.
- Native apps. A web page that plays music and sells tea does not need an app.
- The marketplace. See §2.

## 7. Legal and ethical, before money changes hands

1. **"Non-commercial tribute" stops being true the day you sell anything.** That
   footer is currently doing real work for you. Rewrite it honestly rather than
   leave a line that's become a lie.
2. **Never paywall embedded YouTube content.** Commerce alongside it is fine;
   charging for access to it is not.
3. **Get written permission from living artists** before featuring them
   commercially. An email saying yes is enough, and most will say yes gladly.
4. **Pay the makers first and visibly.** In a small community, your reputation is
   the business. One maker who feels shorted in Kalimpong ends this.
5. **GST registration** once you sell physical goods in India. Talk to an
   accountant before the first sale, not after.

## 8. Honest numbers

All estimates, all soft.

- Darjeeling + Kalimpong + Sikkim: roughly 2.5–3M people
- Global Nepali speakers: ~30M, but most are not your audience
- Realistic addressable core — hill identity, online, some disposable income:
  **200k–500k people**
- If it spreads well through WhatsApp: **50k–200k visits in year one**
- Shop conversion at 0.5–1.5%, average order ₹1,500–2,500

That maths lands somewhere between **₹10L and ₹40L of year-two revenue** if the
shop works and you're diligent. It is not a venture-scale company and you should
stop trying to make it one. It is something better suited to what it is: a
profitable small business that doubles as a cultural institution, with no
investors telling you to add an algorithmic feed.

If you want the venture-scale version, the company is "e-commerce for Himalayan
makers" and the music site is a marketing channel. That's a real business, but
it's a logistics company, and you should know that going in.

## 9. What I'd do if it were mine

1. Pay an illustrator from the hills this month. Fix §3 while the site is small
   enough that nobody has noticed.
2. Add the new music sections. Message every living artist featured. Not to ask
   for anything — just to tell them it exists.
3. Ship song stories and let the community fill them. Become a place, not a page.
4. Sell prints. One product, high margin, no logistics, tests whether this
   audience will buy anything at all.
5. Apply for one archive grant.
6. Only if steps 3–4 work, open the shop with 20 items.
7. Revisit the marketplace in two years, or never.

The domain: **bhanjyang.art** is a fine second domain, but get **bhanjyang.com**
if it's free, and **.in** if you'll sell domestically — Indian buyers trust it.
Point them all at the same place.

The riskiest thing you can do right now is add commerce to a site that has not
yet proved anyone will come back to it twice. Get retention first. The money is
downstream of that, and it will still be there.
